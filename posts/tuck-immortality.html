Angus Tuck’s Next Life: The Cost of Immortality (1893)

What is Angus Tuck’s long-term reaction to immortality? Part 2 of a three-part character journal examining the physical mechanics of hidden survival, the psychological toll of endless waiting, and the theological frameworks that help make sense of a life with no ending.

The first post in this series established Angus Tuck’s eighteenth-century origins. This entry traces his narrative through the novel and musical adaptation, beginning the morning Winnie Foster enters his kitchen and concluding decades later when he and Mae make their final journey back to Treegap.

The Paralysis of an Unchanging Man

The story opens at the spring in 1808 before jumping forward eighty-five years to introduce the Angus the audience spends the show with: late-nineteenth-century Angus, asleep at dawn while Mae dresses to ride out and meet their sons. He is deep in a recurring dream of heaven, one where his family is restored, intact, and has never heard of Treegap. When Mae wakes him to say goodbye, his very first line in the role is a complaint about being pulled out of it: he was having that dream again.

His depression predates the story itself. Angus has been carrying it for decades by the time the audience meets him.

What brought on this paralysis is a slow accumulation of unresolved losses. Farm life originally gave Angus a sense of forward momentum. That momentum collapsed after the family drank from the spring in 1808. Because aging is gradual, the Tucks likely farmed for years before their static physical state became impossible to ignore, leaving Angus to process an impossible reality long before he ever understood its cause.

The true rupture in Angus’s world came when Miles’s wife realized her husband was not aging and fled with their children. That single loss destroyed any illusion that the family could safely stay in one place or build a future. Every relationship Angus attempted afterward ended the same way, with friends aging and dying while he remained fixed in place.

None of this grief ever resolves. Lacking funerals, endings, or any natural season for mourning to lift, the weight accumulates.

Learned Helplessness on the Wheel

When Mae tells Angus there is no point in dreaming because nothing changes, she defines his core psychological state: complete passive withdrawal. Angus has stopped trying to alter his circumstances because every effort over the better part of a century has failed to change his reality.

Mae and his quiet routines are all that anchor him. As the one constant relationship spanning over a century, Mae is the sole partner with whom he can process this existence.

Sleep is his ultimate escape. His years spent lying on the couch waiting for their sons to return demonstrate the depth of his withdrawal. His state of prolonged numbness deepens the moment each brief family reunion ends and the house falls silent again.

The Calvinist Heaven

Angus dreams of a specific, historical resting place. Formed by late eighteenth-century New England Congregationalism, his view of paradise remains strictly agrarian Calvinist: green pastures, the final end of labor, and a soul “asleep in Jesus.” Heaven is the ultimate restoration of the natural order, the moment the weary gather back to their people and rest.

By drinking from the spring, Angus extracted his family from that natural sequence of birth, death, and divine judgment. This creates a terrifying theological crisis for a man grounded in Calvinist doctrine. He is trapped between two possibilities: either his physical transit to salvation is merely delayed, or his unnatural state has permanently disqualified his soul from grace. If a sovereign God permitted this spring to exist, the religious framework of his youth collapses. His dream of heaven remains the last space where his original faith survives.

Decades of unchanging existence gradually erode those original doctrines, driving him into a weary agnosticism. The sheer repetition of unyielding years forces him to confront a universe operating on cold, mechanical repetition. God’s absolute silence in the face of his family’s eternal isolation renders traditional Providence impossible to believe. In place of a structured heavenly reward, he adopts a functional view of existence: the world moves in a blind, endless cycle of natural growth and decay, leaving him a broken gear stuck outside the mechanism. His dream of heaven ceases to be a literal theological destination, becoming a simple, secular desire for final sleep.

The Cottage Arrival

Miles and Jesse bring Winnie into the cottage. Angus reacts first with terror, fearing their secret is out, then with total disbelief. He tells her directly: “There’s just no words to tell you how happy I am to see you. It’s the finest thing that’s happened in . . .” He stops before measuring the years. A growing child in his static home shatters his emotional guard.

When Jesse trips and fires his rifle, Angus takes a bullet to the arm and simply swears in mild annoyance. Physical risk evaporates. Without the threat of injury or death, standard mortal stakes disappear, shifting the entire narrative threat from physical harm to permanent stagnation.

Practicing being a father again

While Mae and Winnie talk upstairs, an ensemble flashback carries Mae back to the day Angus proposed, a memory the book never mentions: he’s young, smitten, eager to dance, a version of him that existed before the water. It’s the only unguarded, romantic color anywhere in the role, and it works because of the contrast. This is who he was, building toward a life.

Later, on the porch at night, he catches Winnie and Jesse sneaking off to the Fair. He has every reason to stop them. Instead he softens the moment Winnie mentions that her father loved the Fair, and he lets them go, asking only for a funnel cake in return. It’s a small choice that does real work: he picks empathy over the rule.

Act Two Urgency

Jesse confesses a stranger spotted him, exposing the family and forcing another sudden relocation. The loss hits Angus on two distinct levels. Practically, it destroys another rare sanctuary. Morally, it emboldens Jesse to push seventeen-year-old Winnie toward drinking the water and staying with him forever.

Mae admits her envy of Jesse’s forward-looking hope, and Angus reassures her of his love. She then hands him the central burden: Winnie needs a father, and Angus must be the one to speak to her.

That request carries structural weight. With Winnie’s father already dead (unlike the novel, where both of her suffocatingly protective parents are alive), Mae asks Angus to fill an existing void. Angus needs no convincing. The same protective instinct that kept his family hidden for eighty-five years already compels him toward this conversation. Mae simply gives him the nudge.

Outside the Turning Wheel

Out on the pond, Angus uses the water and the rowboat to frame life as a wheel that never stops turning. The world keeps rowing, growing, changing, and moving on, but the Tucks have been thrown off the mechanism entirely. They aren’t frozen in a motionless world; the world is moving past them while they remain stuck. His case to Winnie is a direct warning against self-ejection. “You can’t have living without dying,” he tells her in the novel. “So you can’t call it living, what we got. We just are, we just be, like rocks beside the road.”

The stage adaptation preserves this core mechanical truth in “The Wheel,” where Angus articulates his desperation to get back into the cycle, explicitly stating he would “climb back on the wheel” even if it means accepting the end, just to participate in the natural progression of life again. The rowboat scene isn’t a speech about the beauty of death; it’s an urgent warning to a child not to step off the only ride that matters before she has even begun it.

The Banquet Window

The Man in the Yellow Suit’s arrival at the spring is the culmination of a lifelong obsession, fueled by his grandmother’s folklore and driven by commercial intent: secure the wood, control the water, and monetize eternal life. Winnie’s disappearance merely hands him the immediate leverage needed to trade her return for the land.

When he draws a gun at the spring, Mae kills him. In Babbitt’s text, this is a deliberate, brutal blow with a rifle stock; the stage adaptation softens this into an accidental shooting during a physical struggle for the weapon. Checking the pulse, Angus reacts with envy rather than relief, like a starving man watching a dinner banquet through a window. He is looking at a man who just achieved the one exit denied to the Tucks.

The Tucks were never in real physical danger at the spring. The true threat was a dual horror: the commercialization of Winnie’s finite future, and Angus’s realization that even his enemy gets the mercy of an ending while he remains trapped in the static present.

The Mitigation of Risk

A body that cannot die cannot escape through death. In the wrong hands, a nineteenth-century showman looking to bottle the water, a twentieth-century institution running quiet experiments, a Tuck has no natural limit. There is no biological failure available to protect them from being used past human endurance, and no death to end the experiment.

If the spring became public knowledge, the immediate response would be a desperate run on the water, ending in hoarding and violence. It would grant only what the Tucks themselves endure, not the eternal life people imagine. That burden defines the envy on Angus’s face when he looks at the dead body at his feet.

The stage adaptation neuters this tension. Constable Joe arrives, Angus offers a handshake and a plain “we were just passing through,” and Joe chooses to believe an eleven-year-old rather than press the matter. The family is simply told to leave.

In Babbitt’s text, this moment is the climax. The Man in Yellow does not die instantly. The constable sees Mae strike him and arrests her on the spot, holding her in the Treegap jail while the town waits to see if he survives. His death that night turns the charge to murder, sentencing Mae to the gallows.

The real horror is specific: if the town hangs Mae in public and she doesn’t die, the secret is exposed to everyone at once.

To prevent this, the family breaks her out during a thunderstorm. Miles works her cell window loose while Winnie takes Mae’s place under a blanket on the cot, buying the Tucks the hours they need to disappear.

That sequence earns two things the musical skips:

  • Winnie’s AgencyThe swap is Winnie’s idea. Lying in a jail cell for a family she has known for days takes real courage, turning her from a witness into an accomplice.
  • The Actual FarewellThe rain-drenched exchange at the window is the only goodbye the circumstances allow. Every hug and kiss carries the weight of a permanent parting.

By replacing this breach with Constable Joe’s convenience, the adaptation trades high-stakes necessity for an easy exit.

When Angus tells Winnie she reminded them they still have something to live for, the line needs the pressure of the jailbreak behind it. Delivered after a casual police dismissal instead, the farewell lands rushed rather than earned.

The Exodus and Seventy Years in the Margins

When Constable Joe banishes the Tucks from Treegap, he enforces a physical dispersal that fundamentally alters how the family survives. The four scatter, with Angus and Mae staying together while Miles and Jesse wander independently, maintaining their existence through brief, ten-year reunions. Losing Treegap as their anchor forces their reunions into anonymous, temporary locations instead. What they lose across the seven decades between their flight and their quiet return to the cemetery is a distinct, accumulating form of grief.

Sociological frameworks clarify the exact nature of what the Tucks endure during this exile:

  • Ambiguous LossCoined by therapist Pauline Boss, the term describes grief lacking resolution or closure, typical for families of missing persons. This defines the permanent rupture between Miles and his family, Thomas and Anna. Without funerals, graves, or news of their deaths, Miles lives in a permanent state of unconfirmed loss.
  • Disenfranchised GriefFormulated by Kenneth Doka, this applies to loss that cannot be publicly acknowledged or openly mourned. Because the Tucks cannot reveal their condition without risking exposure, their losses, including lost children, abandoned homes, and severed ties, must remain entirely hidden.
  • Chronic SorrowFirst identified by Simon Olshansky, this refers to a continuous, non-resolving grief that resurfaces in response to specific triggers. This is the exact rhythm of Angus’s existence. Every ten years, the reunion does not bring relief. It is a scheduled trigger, renewing his grief at the sight of his frozen, permanently broken sons.

While the family remains static, the infrastructure of the country around them accelerates. The Spanish-American War and the Progressive Era give way to World War I, where national draft registration poses a concrete, legal threat to Miles and Jesse that simple town rumors of witchcraft never did. They navigate the 1918 influenza pandemic, Prohibition, and the rise of the automobile. The Great Depression reshapes the labor landscape around them: their condition makes them immune to starvation or exposure, but the collapse of informal, cash-in-hand day labor makes it harder to blend into a transient workforce.

The passage of the Social Security Act in 1935 introduces a far more dangerous threat than local superstition: bureaucratic tracking. A nine-digit identifier linked to a permanent record, birth date, and employment history directly undermines their primary survival strategy.

By the mid-twentieth century, the nature of their peril flips entirely. In 1808, an unaging person raised fears of the supernatural. By 1940, the primary threat is paper documentation: driver’s licenses, draft cards, tax records, hospital files. Staying on the margins with a horse and wagon stops being a quaint preference; it becomes a functional necessity, the only remaining mode of transit that requires no state-issued identity.

Obsolescence and the Open Road

When Angus and Mae return to Treegap around 1963, it is their first time back since Constable Joe ordered them out. They arrive as they have traveled everywhere for decades: by horse and wagon. The America they roll into is deep in the Cold War, at the height of the Civil Rights Movement, and wired for real-time television broadcasts, a world utterly incompatible with their 1808 origins.

A horse-drawn wagon operating side by side with mid-century automobiles highlights their mechanical isolation, yet it also grants them an accidental cover. Plain-living groups like the Amish had already normalized horse-and-buggy travel on modern roads. That familiar cultural archetype lets the Tucks pass through populated areas without raising alarm, trading on the assumption of religious separatism to dodge the state-issued paper trail that would otherwise expose their lack of aging.

In the novel, this moment belongs to Angus alone. Mae waits in the wagon while he enters the cemetery by himself:

Tuck’s heart quickened. He had noticed the cemetery on the way in. Mae had seen it, too. They had not spoken about it. But both knew it might hold other answers. Tuck straightened his old jacket. He passed through an archway of wrought-iron curlicues, and paused, squinting at the weedy rows of gravestones. And then, far over to the right, he saw a tall monument, once no doubt imposing but now tipped slightly sidewise. On it was carved one name: Foster.

Slowly, Tuck turned his footsteps toward the monument. And saw, as he approached, that there were other, smaller markers all around it. A family plot. And then his throat closed. For it was there. He had wanted it to be there, but now that he saw it, he was overcome with sadness. He knelt and read the inscription:

In Loving Memory
Winifred Foster Jackson
Dear Wife
Dear Mother
1870–1948

“So,” said Tuck to himself. “Two years. She’s been gone two years.” He stood up and looked around, embarrassed, trying to clear the lump from his throat. But there was no one to see him. The cemetery was very quiet. In the branches of a willow behind him, a red-winged blackbird chirped. Tuck wiped his eyes hastily. Then he straightened his jacket again and drew up his hand in a brief salute. “Good girl,” he said aloud. And then he turned and left the cemetery, walking quickly.

Later, as he and Mae rolled out of Treegap, Mae said softly, without looking at him, “She’s gone?” Tuck nodded. “She’s gone,” he answered.

Natalie Babbitt writes the emotional contradiction directly into the narrative mechanics: “he had wanted it to be there, but now that he saw it, he was overcome with sadness.” The relief of confirmation and the grief of finality occur simultaneously, in the same moment. Angus is proud of her for heeding his warning on the rowboat, choosing the natural progression of life and death rather than stepping off the wheel as his family did. At the same time, he mourns her. To a man who has been alive 187 years, frozen perpetually at forty-five, a full mortal life still reads as brief. The relief does not negate the grief.

The musical adaptation cuts this private solitude. Instead, all four Tucks gather around the grave, Mae reads the headstone aloud, and Angus deflects the emotional weight with a joke: “Expert fisherman.” Miles follows with an explicit summary: “Looks like she led the life we never could.”

While this framing aligns with a stage adaptation that consistently presents Angus as a warmer, more comic character, it trades away the single instance where the source material allows him a quiet, private acknowledgment of what was lost. Flattening that solitary moment into group dialogue and a deflective punchline strips the climax of its mechanical weight.

Moment The book’s language What it establishes
Asleep “Tuck almost never smiled except in sleep.” The depression has been constant for decades.
The dream “I was having that dream again, the good one where we’re all in heaven and never heard of Treegap.” Heaven, for Angus, is defined by never having gone to Treegap at all.
Waking up “What in the world could possibly happen to me?” Invulnerability played as dry, weary humor before anyone treats it as a threat.
Meeting Winnie “There’s just no words to tell you how happy I am to see you. It’s the finest thing that’s happened in . . .” He interrupts himself before naming how long it’s been. He can’t finish the sentence.
Looking at her “An expression there that made her feel like an unexpected present, wrapped in pretty paper and tied with ribbons.” Winnie’s own read on his face, tenderness she isn’t used to receiving.
The self-test “The shot knocked him down. Went into his heart. It had to, the way he aimed. And right on through him. It scarcely even left a mark.” Mae, not Jesse, narrates it. Invulnerability confirmed by the family’s own hand, deliberately, once and for all.
At the lake “His face was pinched with the effort of explaining.” Teaching Winnie this costs him something. It isn’t effortless wisdom.
