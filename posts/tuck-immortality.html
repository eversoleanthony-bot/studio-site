Dramaturgy July 2026 Part 2 of 3

The Cost of Immortality

What is Angus Tuck’s long-term reaction to immortality? Part 2 of a three-part character journal on the danger it creates, the toll it takes, and the older stories that help make sense of a life with no ending.

The first post in this series covered where Angus Tuck comes from. This one follows the narrative of the novel and musical adaptation, from the morning Winnie Foster walks into his kitchen to the day, decades later, he and Mae make their last trip back to Treegap.

Waking up already disappointed

The show opens in 1808 at the spring, then jumps forward eighty-five years to introduce the Angus the audience spends the show with: late-nineteenth-century Angus, asleep at dawn while Mae dresses to ride out and meet the boys. He has a recurring dream of heaven with his family, never having heard of Treegap. Mae wakes him to tell him she’s leaving, and his first line in the role is a complaint about being pulled out of it: he was having that dream again. This depression predates the story itself. Angus has already been carrying it for decades by the time the audience meets him.

What brought it on is not one event but a slow accumulation of losses that never got a chance to resolve. Farm life gave his old existence forward motion: planting toward a harvest, building toward a finished barn, raising sons toward manhood. None of that stopped the week the family drank in 1808. Aging is slow enough that a family wouldn’t notice its own absence season to season, so the Tucks likely farmed on for years before the lack of change became impossible to write off as good health, and pinning the spring itself down as the actual cause, rather than some vaguer explanation, was probably still speculative for the family for decades after that. The real break in the settled, forward-building life came when Miles married and, years in, his wife finally noticed her husband wasn’t aging alongside her and left, taking their son. It’s an open question how clearly even the Tucks themselves understood what was happening by that point. That’s where staying in one place, building toward anything, stopped being safe. Every friend Angus has allowed himself to make in whatever town the family lands in since has, by the nature of the arrangement, aged and died while he stayed exactly the same. None of it resolves. None of it gets a funeral, or an ending, or a season where the grief is allowed to run its course and lift. It only accumulates.

“It’s no use having that dream,” Mae tells him. “Nothing’s going to change.” That’s close to a textbook description of learned helplessness, the concept Martin Seligman identified in the 1960s: repeated exposure to a situation with no controllable outcome produces passive withdrawal rather than continued effort. Angus isn’t sad about one thing. He’s stopped trying to change anything, because nothing he’s done has changed anything for the better part of a century. Woodcarving, fishing, and Mae are what keep him going. Theirs is the one constant partnership in the family, well over a century running by 1893, and whatever coping Angus manages, he does it with her.

Sleep is the other half of the pair, and it’s the one that doesn’t work: not rest, but escape. Mae’s own account of ten years spent lying on the couch waiting for the boys to come home is what that withdrawal looks like from the outside, low-effort and low-motivation, deepening every time a reunion with the boys ends and the house goes quiet again. The specific content of the escape matters too. He doesn’t dream of nothing. He dreams of heaven.

“Heaven” means something exact to a man raised in Angus’s religious world. His inheritance is Reformed, Calvinist-descended New England Congregationalism, plain and unadorned. Rest and reunion dominate the picture: heaven as the end of labor, and as being gathered again with the people who died in the faith, the same idea carried in common period gravestone language: at rest, asleep in Jesus, gathered to his people. A farmer of his background likely pictured it in agrarian terms too, green pastures and still water, the language of Psalm 23. And Calvinist theology carries real anxiety about who is and isn’t among the elect, chosen for salvation. A man who has pulled his entire family out of the natural order that faith assumes, birth, labor, death, judgment, has genuine theological grounds to wonder whether he’s just delayed his death or disqualified himself from the heaven he dreams about every night. That uncertainty cuts both ways. He might still be counted among the elect, just delayed. Or a God who’d let a spring like that exist at all isn’t the God he grew up believing in, and the dream isn’t really about qualifying for heaven so much as being the one place left where he can still afford to believe any of it was ever true.

A child changes everything

Miles and Jesse bring Winnie into the cottage, and Angus’s first reaction is terror, that someone has found the secret, before it turns into something closer to disbelief. The novel gives him the line directly: “There’s just no words to tell you how happy I am to see you. It’s the finest thing that’s happened in . . .” He interrupts himself before he can finish naming how long it’s been. A living, growing child in his kitchen breaks something open in his soul.

In the same scene, Jesse trips, his rifle goes off, and Angus takes the shot square in the arm and pops back up swearing about it, more annoyed than hurt, proof, live, that nothing can touch him. Play it for the joke it is on the surface. The information underneath it is not a joke: a bullet doesn’t stick, a fall doesn’t stick, and ordinary fear, which runs entirely on the possibility of loss and injury, simply stops applying to this family. That single fact quietly changes what danger even means for the rest of the story.

Practicing being a father again

While Mae and Winnie talk upstairs, an ensemble flashback carries Mae back to the day Angus proposed, a memory the book never mentions: he’s young, smitten, eager to dance, a version of him that existed before the water. It’s the only unguarded, romantic color anywhere in the role, and it works because of the contrast. This is who he was, building toward a life.

Later, on the porch at night, he catches Winnie and Jesse sneaking off to the Fair. He has every reason to stop them. Instead he softens the moment Winnie mentions that her father loved the Fair, and he lets them go, asking only for a funnel cake in return. It’s a small choice that does real work: he picks empathy over the rule.

Jesse’s case for staying seventeen

The urgency of the second act starts with Jesse confessing he’s been spotted by a stranger, the Man in Yellow Suit, and it lands on Angus as two problems stacked on top of each other. First, practically: the family has to pack up and abandon a home again, the loss of another sanctuary in a life made of nothing else. Second, and harder: Jesse wants Winnie to drink the water when she turns seventeen, so she can stay with him. Mae, on the porch, admits her own envy of Jesse’s forward-looking hope, and Angus reassures her of his love before turning to the harder task Mae hands him next: she tells him Winnie needs a father, and asks him to be the one to have the serious talk with her. That line carries more weight than it first appears. In the book, both of Winnie’s parents are alive, just suffocatingly protective. Here, her father has already died, so there’s already a father-shaped absence in her life before she ever sits down at the Tucks’ table. Mae is asking him to fill a specific, already-existing gap. In the novel, Angus takes Winnie out on the water on his own initiative. Here, Mae says it out loud first. That doesn’t read as a man needing to be talked into fatherhood. His own caution about the secret, the same instinct that has kept this family alive for eighty-five years, would already have him moving toward this conversation with or without her prompting. Mae’s line reads more as naming what he was already about to do than pushing a reluctant man into unfamiliar territory.

The argument against forever

Out on the lake, fishing, Angus makes his case to Winnie in his own words: “You can’t have living without dying. So you can’t call it living, what we got. We just are, we just be, like rocks beside the road.” And later: “Don’t be afraid of death, Winnie. Be afraid of not being truly alive.” The direction for playing it is not to preach but to teach, using the anchored rowboat itself as the physical shape of what he’s describing. The goal in the scene is narrow and urgent: keep Winnie from making a choice that can never be undone.

A few older stories sharpen exactly what kind of case this is, since Angus isn’t the first character stuck with unending life.

The Wandering Jew

Cursed in medieval Christian legend to walk the earth without dying until the Second Coming, forced into permanent exile so he’s never recognized staying in one place too long, the Wandering Jew is the closest ancestor to the family’s own decade-by-decade relocation. The legend was widespread enough that Angus could plausibly have grown up knowing some version of it.

Tithonus

A useful contrast rather than a match. In the Greek myth, Tithonus is granted eternal life but not eternal youth, so he ages forever instead of never aging, shriveling into something barely human and begging for a death that never comes. Line the two curses up side by side and age was never really the point in either one. Losing an ending is.

The Struldbrugs

Jonathan Swift’s Struldbrugs, from Gulliver’s Travels, published in 1726 and popular enough that a literate farmer of Angus’s generation could have encountered it, are immortal but keep aging into senility, pitied and stripped of civil rights once they turn eighty. Swift arrives at the same conclusion Angus does on his own: life without vitality isn’t a gift, and no society has a working place for people outside its normal life cycle.

The Fountain of Youth

Ponce de León’s Fountain of Youth is the legend the family’s own story answers back to directly, the wish granted and followed all the way through instead of stopping at the romantic promise.

Sisyphus

Pushing his boulder up the hill only to watch it roll back down forever, Sisyphus is the closest match for the actual texture of the Tucks’ eternity: not dramatic suffering, just repetition without progress. Camus’s 1942 essay reframes that as the defining condition of a meaningless universe and concludes that one must imagine Sisyphus happy, since meaning has to be built inside the repetition itself rather than found at the end of it. That’s a live option for Angus. His woodcarving, his fishing, the life he still shares with Mae, read as his own version of imagining Sisyphus happy.

The man who wanted what they have

By the time he reaches the spring, the Man in Yellow Suit has been chasing this for most of his life, not just since Jesse got spotted at the fair. He grew up on his grandmother’s old story about a family that never ages, and once he’s close enough to be sure the Tucks are real, his plan is plain and mercenary: buy his way onto the wood, find the source, and sell eternal life to whoever can pay for it. Winnie disappearing with the family hands him the leverage he needs. He can trade her safe return for the spring itself, or simply hold her against the Tucks until they give him what he wants. That’s the position he’s in when he corners them.

He pulls a gun on Winnie at the spring. Mae strikes him with the rifle stock and kills him. Angus shields Winnie, checks the body’s pulse, and the novel is specific about what a scene like this does to his face: not relief, but something “entranced and—yes, envious—like a starving man looking through a window at a banquet.” He is looking at a man who just did the one thing he cannot do.

That envy is the honest payoff of the invulnerability Jesse’s rifle proved as a joke back in Act One. The real danger in this story was never to the Tucks themselves. It runs in two directions instead.

The risk to the family

A body that can’t die also can’t escape through death. In the wrong hands, a nineteenth-century showman looking to bottle the water, a twentieth-century institution running quiet experiments, a Tuck has no version of the ordinary limit that protects a normal body from being used past its breaking point, with no death available to end it.

The risk to everyone else

If the spring itself became public knowledge, in any era, the realistic response is a run on the water, ending in hoarding and violence over who controls it and a population that keeps adding people without ever losing any. It would not even give people what they think they’re buying. It would give them what the Tucks have: stasis without progress, no ending that lets any story or generation finish. Angus stands as the only line of defense against something closer to civilizational collapse, with no ally or institution to share that weight with.

That’s what the envy on his face is about.

Constable Joe arrives, Angus offers a good handshake and a plain “we were just passing through,” and Joe chooses to believe an eleven-year-old over pressing the matter, so the family is simply told to leave and never come back.

The novel handles this very differently, and it is the actual climax of the book. The Man in Yellow doesn’t die on the spot. The constable sees Mae strike him and arrests her right there, holding her in Treegap’s jail while the town waits to find out if the man lives. He dies of his injuries that night, which turns the charge into murder and Mae into a woman sentenced to hang for it. The real horror is specific: if the town hangs Mae in public and she doesn’t die, the secret is exposed to everyone at once. So the family breaks her out the night before, during a thunderstorm, Miles working her cell window loose while Jesse leads Winnie to the jailhouse. Winnie takes Mae’s place in the cell, lying under the blanket on her cot so the escape isn’t discovered until morning, buying the family the hours they need to disappear for good. Before she climbs in, every one of the Tucks hugs and kisses her goodbye.

That sequence earns two things this adaptation skips past. The plan is Winnie’s own idea. She’s the one who offers to take Mae’s place, and volunteering to lie in a jail cell for a family she’s known a matter of days is real courage, built on real love for them. It’s also the actual goodbye. Every hug and kiss at that jailhouse window is the family and Winnie saying farewell to each other in the only way their circumstances allow, all four Tucks and Winnie together one last time, with a whole novel’s worth of feeling underneath it that never makes it onstage. Constable Joe simply choosing to believe an eleven-year-old and letting a family connected to a killing walk out of town is a harder sell by comparison, a shortcut that trades the novel’s real stakes, and its real farewell, for a tidier ending. The show is weaker for the trade.

Angus’s goodbye to Winnie, that she reminded them they’ve still got something to live for, is played plain rather than sentimental, a father saying goodbye to the one person outside his family who ever fully understood what they are, knowing that understanding changes nothing: he still has to leave, and still can never come back to find out how her life turns out. Without the jailbreak behind it, this goodbye feels rushed. The audience deserves a fuller farewell than this version gives them.

The exodus, and the seventy years after

The family scatters, Angus and Mae together, Miles and Jesse apart, still reconvening once a decade the way they always have. Constable Joe’s order never to come back makes Treegap itself too dangerous to keep using as the meeting point, so wherever the four of them gather now, it isn’t the wood anymore. What that costs them across the seventy years between this exodus and their eventual return to Treegap is its own kind of grief.

Ambiguous loss

A term from family therapist Pauline Boss, developed studying families of missing soldiers and later people caring for relatives with dementia. It describes grief with no resolution because the person is gone without confirmation of death, exactly Miles and Thomas: no funeral, no closure, no way to ever know.

Disenfranchised grief

From researcher Kenneth Doka. It describes loss that can’t be openly mourned because it isn’t socially acknowledged, the whole family’s situation, since they can never tell anyone the real story of what they’ve lost without exposing the secret.

Chronic sorrow

From Simon Olshansky’s work with families raising children with permanent disabilities. It describes grief that never fully resolves and instead resurfaces predictably, which is the exact shape of what happens to Angus every ten years: a recurring wave of the same grief, retriggered on schedule by the sight of his own broken son.

The world they’re hiding inside of changes faster than the family does. The Spanish-American War and the Progressive Era give way to the First World War and the first national draft, meaning evading military registration becomes a real practical danger for Miles and Jesse rather than a rumor of witchcraft, followed by the 1918 to 1919 influenza pandemic, then Prohibition and the automobile boom.

The Depression hits an itinerant, cash-poor family especially hard, since the odd day labor and seasonal work they depend on dries up nationwide. The Social Security Act of 1935 is arguably one of the biggest threats the family has ever faced, a nine-digit number tied to a name and a birth date, expected on nearly every job, a permanent record in exactly the shape they’ve spent over a century avoiding. By this point the danger the family faces has quietly flipped. In 1808, an unaging family risked being read as cursed. Now the far bigger risk is paperwork, driver’s licenses, vehicle registration, draft cards, hospital records, none of which survive a family that can’t produce a consistent birth date decade over decade. Staying with a horse and wagon is a practical necessity by this point, the only form of transportation left that doesn’t require documentation.

The Second World War brings a second draft crisis. The postwar boom, suburbanization, and the interstate highway system beginning in 1956 accelerate a version of America that has almost nothing left in common with 1808. Television arrives in most homes by the late 1950s.

The return, in the age of automobiles

By the time Angus and Mae make it back to Treegap, around 1963, it’s the first time either of them has set foot there since the family was ordered out, and they arrive the way they’ve arrived everywhere for decades: by horse and wagon. This isn’t a stop on the usual decade rotation. They’ve come specifically to find out what happened to Winnie. The country they’re rolling into is deep in the Cold War, the Civil Rights Movement is at its peak, and a man can watch the news of the day arrive instantly into his own living room, a fact that would have sounded like witchcraft to the same man in 1808.

A stranger mocking them from a car window at a gas station has no idea he’s looking at a deliberate, safer choice of transportation for this couple. There’s even a strange upside in it: a plain-living family that travels by horse and wagon has a ready-made explanation available by this era that didn’t exist in 1808. They can pass, at a glance, for a plain-living or religiously separatist family, a category most Americans by now already have in mind, even if New Hampshire itself isn’t Amish country the way Pennsylvania or Ohio are. That assumption is useful cover. The tradeoff is that the family can borrow the appearance of people who’ve opted out of modern life, but they can never opt in. A license, a car, any of it, requires exactly the paper trail that would end them.

In the novel, this moment belongs to Angus alone. Mae waits in the wagon while he walks into the cemetery by himself:

Tuck’s heart quickened. He had noticed the cemetery on the way in. Mae had seen it, too. They had not spoken about it. But both knew it might hold other answers. Tuck straightened his old jacket. He passed through an archway of wrought-iron curlicues, and paused, squinting at the weedy rows of gravestones. And then, far over to the right, he saw a tall monument, once no doubt imposing but now tipped slightly sidewise. On it was carved one name: Foster.

Slowly, Tuck turned his footsteps toward the monument. And saw, as he approached, that there were other, smaller markers all around it. A family plot. And then his throat closed. For it was there. He had wanted it to be there, but now that he saw it, he was overcome with sadness. He knelt and read the inscription:

In Loving Memory
Winifred Foster Jackson
Dear Wife
Dear Mother
1870–1948

“So,” said Tuck to himself. “Two years. She’s been gone two years.” He stood up and looked around, embarrassed, trying to clear the lump from his throat. But there was no one to see him. The cemetery was very quiet. In the branches of a willow behind him, a red-winged blackbird chirped. Tuck wiped his eyes hastily. Then he straightened his jacket again and drew up his hand in a brief salute. “Good girl,” he said aloud. And then he turned and left the cemetery, walking quickly.

Later, as he and Mae rolled out of Treegap, Mae said softly, without looking at him, “She’s gone?” Tuck nodded. “She’s gone,” he answered.

Babbitt puts the duality right in the text instead of leaving it for an actor to infer: “he had wanted it to be there, but now that he saw it, he was overcome with sadness.” Wanting confirmation and being leveled by it are the same sentence, not two separate beats. He’s proud of her, genuinely, for staying on the wheel he described to her at the lake, choosing an ordinary life and an ordinary death instead of drinking the water and dropping off it the way his own family did, and he’s grieving her, just as genuinely, since a full lifetime still reads as young to a man who’s been alive 187 years at this point, thirteen shy of two full centuries and still frozen at forty-five. Neither feeling cancels the other.

This production cuts that private version of the scene. All four Tucks arrive together, Mae reads the headstone aloud, and Angus’s response is a dry, deflecting joke, “Expert fisherman,” rather than a man alone with a lump in his throat. Miles follows with, “Looks like she led the life we never could.” It plays, and it’s an honest choice for a production that’s spent two acts building Angus warmer and funnier than the novel’s version. The musical trades away the one moment the book gives him to be quietly, privately serious about what he lost, and that’s a real loss for the audience.

In the book’s own words

Every line below is Natalie Babbitt’s own words, checked directly against the novel, gathered here as the given circumstances underneath the scenes above.

Moment The book’s language What it establishes
Asleep “Tuck almost never smiled except in sleep.” The depression has been constant for decades.
The dream “I was having that dream again, the good one where we’re all in heaven and never heard of Treegap.” Heaven, for Angus, is defined by never having gone to Treegap at all.
Waking up “What in the world could possibly happen to me?” Invulnerability played as dry, weary humor before anyone treats it as a threat.
Meeting Winnie “There’s just no words to tell you how happy I am to see you. It’s the finest thing that’s happened in . . .” He interrupts himself before naming how long it’s been. He can’t finish the sentence.
Looking at her “An expression there that made her feel like an unexpected present, wrapped in pretty paper and tied with ribbons.” Winnie’s own read on his face, tenderness she isn’t used to receiving.
The self-test “The shot knocked him down. Went into his heart. It had to, the way he aimed. And right on through him. It scarcely even left a mark.” Mae, not Jesse, narrates it. Invulnerability confirmed by the family’s own hand, deliberately, once and for all.
At the lake “His face was pinched with the effort of explaining.” Teaching Winnie this costs him something. It isn’t effortless wisdom.
The Wheel “We just are, we just be, like rocks beside the road.” His own precise definition of what the family lost.
Watching him die “It was as if he were entranced and—yes, envious—like a starving man looking through a window at a banquet.” The one moment his composure breaks, watching a man do what he cannot.
