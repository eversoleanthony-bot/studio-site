Part 1: Angus Tuck’s First Life: New Hampshire, 1763–1808

Tuck Everlasting is a musical by Chris Miller (music), Nathan Tysen (lyrics), and Claudia Shear and Tim Federle (book), based on the 1975 novel by Natalie Babbitt. It tells the story of Winnie Foster, an eleven-year-old girl who stumbles onto the Tuck family’s secret in the woods outside her town: a spring that stops anyone who drinks from it from ever aging or dying again. Winnie has to decide what to do with that secret, and whether to drink from the spring herself, while a stranger looking to exploit the spring for his own profit closes in on the family.

This is the first of three posts exploring the social, political, and economic pressures that shaped Angus Tuck (my role for an October 2026 production with Sandy Arts Guild) and his family through their extraordinary situation, the specific given circumstances laid out scene-by-scene in both Natalie Babbitt’s novel and the stage adaptation, the real nuances of the family’s relationships, and the risk and stakes of being forced to remain anonymous to their larger communities. I’m making my process public mostly as a form of accountability, but who knows? Maybe it will help another actor, director, or dramaturg at some point down the line. It’s a wheel, after all.

The Forty-Five Years That Built Him

Though the source material never gives an exact birth year, it’s clear Angus was in his forties when he and his family drank from the spring in Treegap wood. I’m forty-five this year, so I built this character study around the assumption that Angus was my exact age when he drank and stopped aging for good. That puts his birth year at 1763, forty-five years before 1808.

Working from that year, Angus is born the same year the French and Indian War ends. He was born into a rural New Hampshire farming family under British rule. The Congregationalist Church shaped nearly every local institution. Colonial resentment foreshadowing a conflict builds through his entire boyhood, absorbed secondhand through his father, his church, and town gossip rather than anything he understands firsthand.

Boyhood on the Crown’s Edge

His childhood runs through a decade of rising British taxes and trade restrictions, on sugar and molasses, on the paper behind every deed and will, on glass and paint and tea, each of these caused his family financial hardships in the form of higher prices and scarcer coin. It led to a steady, close-to-the-ground resentment of a government an ocean away that kept taxing without representation. By the time he’s eleven, Portsmouth has its own answer to the Boston Tea Party, patriots turning tea ships away rather than letting them unload, proof to a household like his that this kind of resistance is a regional endeavor and that it can work.

A boy raised on that particular, steady drip of resentment learns that distant authority and the paperwork it runs on are things to fear and avoid. That lesson outlives the Revolution by a long way. It’s the same instinct, decades later, that leads Angus, a man with an impossible secret, to keep steering clear of every form, record, and official who might ask a question he can’t answer safely.

Day to day life for Angus in the 1760s means waking before dawn to do chores: milking, feeding stock, hauling water and firewood. He may have had a typical breakfast of cornmeal mush, brown bread, and maybe salt pork or eggs. A boy his age spends most of his working hours under his father’s eye, learning to handle tools, tend livestock, and read weather and soil. What schooling he gets comes in short winter terms at a one-room schoolhouse, a hornbook and the Bible for textbooks, squeezed into the weeks when the heaviest field labor lets up. Church is an important part of his routine: a two or three hour Sunday service, catechism drilled at home in between, family prayer and a chapter of scripture most evenings before bed. Play is whatever time is left over: meeting other children at the husking bees and quilting bees (traditional communal gatherings that combined heavy seasonal or domestic work with social celebration, turning mandatory chores into festive community events), swimming in summer, sledding in winter. Respect for elders is enforced daily: at the table, and at meeting.

The house he grows up in is a one or two room farmhouse built around a single hearth, the only heat and stove in the household, walls chinked with clay and moss against the cold. Medicine is home remedies and prayer, a doctor only for the direst emergencies if one is even reachable. Childhood illness is a constant, quiet threat, and losing a sibling in infancy is common rather than the exception. Light after dark comes from the fire and a few rationed tallow candles, so the household’s rhythm bends to daylight.

Growing Up in a Crumbling Empire

Angus is twelve when the Revolutionary War starts and twenty when it ends, so he can’t remember a world without it. New Hampshire militia under General John Stark fought decisively at Bennington in 1777, when he’s fourteen, an event he would have heard about in vivid detail long before he was old enough to be called up himself.

Wartime pressure tightens daily life through these years. With older men and some of the community’s young men away with the militia or the Continental Army, more of the farm work falls to boys his age, alongside women and grandfathers. Manufactured goods, cloth, tea, sugar, gunpowder, and iron tools, grow scarce and expensive as British trade is cut off. Homespun cloth and a diet made almost entirely of what the farm itself produces becomes common fare. Salt, essential for preserving meat through the winter, is in particularly short supply. Continental paper currency loses value fast and daily trade reverts to bartering for goods. News of the war travels by post rider and hand-copied newspaper and reaches a farm this far inland weeks after the fact. The war is lived through rumor and anxious waiting.

Coming of Age in the New Republic

By the time the country is inventing its own government, he’s a young man building a household of his own. Angus comes of age at twenty into postwar depression across New England. Shays’ Rebellion breaks out in neighboring Massachusetts in 1786 when he’s twenty-three. The Constitutional Convention meets in 1787, the year he turns twenty-four. New Hampshire ratifies the Constitution as the ninth state in June 1788, the vote that puts the federal government into effect, when he’s twenty-five. Washington’s presidency begins the following year.

This is the likely window for meeting and marrying Mae and for Miles and Jesse’s births. The Tucks are a young family built inside the first uncertain years of the American experiment. Courtship runs through barn dances, huskings, and church socials. He marries Mae the first week of August 1784 at the age of twenty-one. Miles is born in 1786 and Jesse five years later in 1791.

Building an independent household meant clearing timber, building a cabin by hand, and breaking new ground with an ox-drawn plow. Years of physical labor were a reality before a farm could actually support a family.

Marriage brought an immediate division of labor. Angus worked the fields, cleared timber, built fences, and tended livestock. Mae ran the house, managed the garden, spun and wove cloth, and handled the dairy. She took on the relentless seasonal work of preserving enough food to survive a New Hampshire winter: salting, smoking, drying, and pickling, all while making the family’s soap and tallow candles from rendered fat.

By 1808, remaining on a New Hampshire farm meant managing an ever-shrinking plot of rocky soil, divided thinner with every passing generation. Word of families leaving for Vermont, western New York, or the rich frontier soil of Ohio, a phenomenon contemporaries called “Ohio fever,” dominated rural New England dinner tables. Regional land scarcity was already pushing men of Angus’s generation west, but the Embargo Act of 1807 accelerated the crisis.

When Jefferson halted maritime trade, the local economy collapsed. Essential manufactured goods like iron for tools and salt for meat preservation became scarce and wildly expensive overnight. For a family with two growing sons, self-sufficiency required more acreage than New Hampshire could offer. The decision to pack a wagon in 1808 was a direct response to these combined pressures. When Angus, Mae, Miles, and Jesse ride into the Treegap woods looking for a fresh start, they are simply part of a larger migration of farm families searching for viable land before they stumble onto the spring.

(Note on timeline: Natalie Babbitt’s original novel sets their journey fifteen years earlier, in 1793. Because the stage adaptation moves the main action to 1893, the family’s origin shifts forward to 1808. Every date in this study builds on the musical’s timeline rather than the book’s.)

The Soundscape of the Tucks

The musical language of Tuck Everlasting is anchored directly in the physical and oral traditions of late 18th-century New England. For a farming family in Angus’s era, music was an active, communal force rather than a passive performance. English, Irish, and Scottish folk ballads, fiddle tunes at barn dances, and the driving, four-part harmonies of New England shape-note singing formed the daily soundscape of his youth. Composer Chris Miller and lyricist Nathan Tysen deliberately tapped into this heritage, drawing on their deep love of American folk music to build a score rich in acoustic strings, modal melodies, and driving rhythm. When reviewers point to the folksy, almost Celtic energy of “The Wheel” or the foot-pounding momentum of the company numbers, they are hearing the exact sonic DNA of Angus’s world, a musical landscape that feels rugged, communal, and tied to the soil.

Outside of song, creative expression lived in the physical work of human hands. Woodcarving, the craft that follows Angus through the rest of his immortal life, sits directly inside this regional tradition of practical folk art. For a working man of his time, shaping timber was a natural extension of daily labor, requiring an intimate understanding of grain, growth, and seasonal change. In the context of the story, Angus’s carving is a tactile, deeply human outlet, serving as a physical process that keeps him anchored to the natural cycle of life and time, even after time stops for him.

The Splintering

A decade after drinking from the spring, before the reality of their condition fully settles in, Miles marries and has a son, Thomas. At first, the lack of physical change is subtle enough to blur into the background of seasonal labor. Eventually, the gap becomes impossible to ignore. In a world that interprets the unexplained through a deeply religious framework, an unaging husband registers as something sinister: a curse, an unclean mark, or a bargain with the devil. When Miles’s wife realizes the same uncanny stillness hangs over Angus, Mae, and Jesse, her fear hardens into certainty. Believing the household is cursed, she takes Thomas and flees.

She finds refuge with a trusted friend, seeking safety in a community that validates her dread. Miles never sees his son again, left only with the agonizing silence of not knowing if Thomas lived, aged, or died. Yet the story does not vanish with the broken marriage. That same friend passes the encounter down to her grandson as a terrifying, true bedtime tale about a family untouched by time. Decades later, that boy grows up to be the Man in the Yellow Suit, transforming a forgotten family tragedy into an obsessive hunt. For Angus, watching his eldest son’s life fracture becomes the definitive proof that their immortality is not a gift to be celebrated, but a burden that severs them from the natural human order.

The Mechanics of Isolation

That loss becomes the hard lesson that reshapes how the entire family survives from that point forward, while cementing a rift between the brothers that never fully heals. Remaining together as a group of four unaging adults poses an immediate, slow-motion exposure risk. To survive, a new rule takes hold. Angus and Mae stay together as a pair, maintaining the one constant partnership in the family, while Miles and Jesse go their separate ways. Part of this split is practical logic, as fewer ageless people moving together as a unit reduces suspicion. Part of it is interpersonal friction. Jesse’s restlessness grates against Miles’s guardedness, and eighty-five years is far too long to carry that kind of tension at close range.

All four reconvene on a fixed schedule: exactly once a decade, during the first week of August, at the spring outside Treegap. It stands as the single unmovable point in an otherwise scattered, undocumented existence. For Angus, this arrangement means fatherhood exists almost entirely in fragments. He misses the ordinary texture of a decade with his sons. Instead, he gets a few compressed days once every ten years before they scatter again until the next August.

The toll lands differently on each of them. Miles emerges from his loss guarded and calcified, carrying a grief that goes unprocessed because he lacks the presence of his family to help him work through it. Angus watches his eldest son harden across decades of brief reunions, powerless to intervene when it matters most and able to offer comfort only in short, scheduled windows. Jesse copes through the opposite extreme, refusing to look backward, chasing forward momentum, and treating immortality as an adventure to avoid being flattened by the same grief that consumed his brother.

That cumulative absence is where the final post picks up.

Further Watching & Reading

  • Awake, My Soul: The Story of the Sacred Harp (Matt & Erica Hinton, 2006), a feature documentary on the shape-note singing-school tradition Angus would have grown up inside.
  • Book: Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England by William Cronon. Helpful for understanding the agricultural and environmental pressures (soil exhaustion, deforestation, land scarcity) that forced young families like the Tucks out of early New England and onto the road toward the frontier.
